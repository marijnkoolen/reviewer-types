Dutch parsers for online book reviews: a gold-free comparison

Corpus. data/review_sentences/0 — 670 reviews, 7,125 sentences, ~103k words of user-generated Dutch, one sentence per line as shipped.

Systems. Alpino (official RUG build git20260629, run in Docker), Frog (via python-frog, config ~/.config/frog/nld), Stanza 1.14 (nl, UD Dutch Alpino + LassySmall models), spaCy 3.8 (nl_core_news_lg).

Method. There is no gold standard for this corpus, so nothing below is an accuracy figure. Everything is agreement, structured so that agreement is informative, plus heuristics targeting one specific failure: a lemmatiser emitting a string that is not a Dutch word. Method and code: src/parsers/README.md.

1. How to read these numbers

Three biases, all knowable in direction, apply throughout.

Agreement is not accuracy, and two of the four systems are not independent. Stanza and spaCy are both trained on UD Dutch (Alpino + LassySmall), so they share training data and therefore share errors. Stanza and Alpino share provenance, because UD Dutch Alpino is derived from Alpino’s own treebank. Frog is the only system here independent of the other three, which makes Frog-vs-anything the most informative agreement number in this report, and makes a majority vote over the four systems unusable as a silver standard.

Lemma conventions differ, and the difference is large. Stanza follows UD Dutch Alpino and marks separable particles and compound seams with underscores (op_steken, spel_fout, 16_jarig). Alpino goes further: its lemmas are compound-decomposed citation forms (bestsellerslijsten → bestseller_lijst, paardenfans → paard_fan, zwart-witfoto's → zwart_wit_foto), it normalises spelling (sexmisbruik → seks_misbruik), it emits vanalles → van alles with a space, and it pulls particles in from context (gaat → tegemoet_gaan). Frog and spaCy write plain citation forms. Scoring these against each other measures the annotation guideline, not the lemmatiser, so every figure below is reported twice: exact, and modulo convention (underscores and hyphens stripped, casefolded). This matters: it moved Stanza’s unattested-lemma rate from 3.11% to 0.93% in the three-way run.

The unattested heuristic over-flags. A lemma counts as unattested when its string occurs nowhere else in the corpus — neither as a surface form nor as any other tool’s lemma anywhere. In ~100k tokens many correct citation forms are simply absent. It is a triage signal for manual inspection, not a verdict.

The join key. Every runner emits character offsets into the original review line. Two systems “agree on a token” when they emit the same span, and lemma agreement is only ever computed on spans all four systems segment identically (114,528 of ~115k tokens). Without that restriction a tokenisation difference shows up silently as a lemma difference.

Alpino is the most central tokeniser — it has the highest agreement with every other system despite none of them being trained to match it.

Practical consequence: if you only need tokens, this choice barely matters. Pick on speed.

Agreement runs at ~99.3% on frequent forms and collapses to 67–82% on hapaxes. Since 7,275 of 114,528 tokens are hapaxes and review vocabulary is overwhelmingly rare — compounds, loanwords, names, spelling variants — a single headline agreement figure describes the part of the corpus nobody worries about.

Note that alpino/frog hapax agreement (70.64%) reads worse than frog/spacy (81.98%) largely because of Alpino’s decompounding: canon() strips underscores but cannot undo dropped linking morphemes (paard_fan vs paardenfan). That row understates real agreement.

Per-tool dissent and suspicion flags

Over 102,112 non-punctuation comparable tokens:

tool singleton uncorroborated unattested
frog 1.56% 1.56% 0.348%
alpino 1.69% 1.40% 0.539%
stanza 0.80% 1.07% 0.626%
spacy_lg 1.28% 1.85% 0.962%

singleton = the tool dissents while all others agree with each other; uncorroborated = no other tool proposes this lemma for this token; unattested = the lemma string occurs nowhere else in the corpus.

spaCy’s unattested rate is ~2.8× Frog’s, and its flagged cases are genuine non-words: lotgenoat, familienam, dierenverhaol, kauwgomplaa, werkwijz, opsteekken. Frog’s flagged cases are mostly correct citation forms the small corpus happens not to contain (uitdenken, aftuigen, gieten). That asymmetry is the finding: the same heuristic flags real errors for one system and false alarms for another.

Stanza’s low singleton rate (0.80%) should not be read as accuracy. Stanza sits between Alpino (shared provenance) and spaCy (shared training data), so it rarely dissents alone by construction.

Worth noting: spaCy’s Dutch lemmatizer is not a lookup table. Both nl_core_news_lg and nl_core_news_trf run an EditTreeLemmatizer — a trained component. Its OOV failures are learned suffix-edit rules misfiring on unseen words, which is a data problem, and therefore fixable (§6).

4. Compounds and morphology

Frog’s MBMA is the only morpheme segmentation available. Separating compounding from inflection and derivation properly needs Frog’s deepmorph model, which this installation lacks (deepmorph=True fails to initialise), so the analysis approximates it with an affix stoplist. Treat the split as heuristic.

Of 102,607 non-punctuation tokens with an analysis, 25.7% are multi-segment:

segments tokens share
1 76,210 74.27%
2 18,712 18.24%
3 6,266 6.11%
4 1,219 1.19%
5+ 200 0.19%

By word class, multi-segment rate: VERB 63.9%, ADJ 57.5%, NOUN 46.1%, ADV 13.4%, NUM 21.8%, and 0% for closed classes.

Compound claims (≥2 free-standing members, i.e. not just inflection): 4,865 tokens, 4.7% of analysed tokens. Of those, 2.4% have under half their members attested anywhere in the corpus — and they are overwhelmingly loanwords:

podcast       [pod][cast]
Metamorphosen [metamorp][hose][n]
Endlösung     [endlös][ung]
Ausweis       [aus][weis]
Stromblazers  [strom][blaas][er][s]
kauwgomplaatje[kauw][gomplaat][je]

This is MBMA’s known weakness — it is trained on Dutch and forces Dutch morphology onto English and German material, which online book reviews are full of.

Cross-check: Frog MBMA vs underscore lemmas

Alpino and Stanza both encode compound seams as underscores in the lemma, giving two independent opinions on where the seams are. Because Alpino normalises members to citation form and drops linking morphemes, the check counts members rather than requiring identical strings.

comparison both split same member count only Frog only other
Frog vs Alpino 2,088 94.6% 2,763 1,898
Frog vs Stanza 1,650 93.5% 3,206 1,118

Two systems built on completely different principles — memory-based classification versus a hand-written lexicalised grammar — agree on the number of compound members 94.6% of the time when both decide to split. The real disagreement is about whether to split at all, and Frog splits considerably more often.

If you want compound splitting, Alpino gives it to you in the lemma field for free, and without MBMA’s loanword failure mode, since it is lexicon-driven. Frog’s MBMA remains the richer analysis (full morpheme sequence including affixes), but it needs the loanword cases filtered.

5. Sentence splitting

Document mode (--doc-mode) joins each review’s lines back into running text with single spaces and feeds the whole review to each system, so every splitter has to find the boundaries again. Alpino switches from tokenize_no_breaks.sh to tokenize.sh for this, with a sentinel line between documents to preserve the mapping from its XML output back to reviews.

The reference is not neutral

The corpus’s existing line segmentation was produced by Trankit. Trankit does not reuse Stanza’s splitter — it is a separate XLM-R-based joint tokeniser/splitter — but it is trained on the same UD Dutch treebanks (Alpino + LassySmall) as Stanza’s splitter, and spaCy’s boundaries come from its UD-trained parser. Three of the systems scored here therefore share segmentation conventions with the reference, and their agreement with it is substantially circular.

camp systems relation to the reference
UD-trained stanza, spacy_lg same treebank conventions
rule-based frog, alpino ucto / tokenize.sh; independent

Highest F1 against the reference does not mean best splitter. It partly means “trained on the same data as whatever made the reference”.

Agreement with the existing segmentation

The n- columns are a convention-neutral rescoring that drops every boundary following a colon or semicolon (370 of them) from both sides.

alpino rule-based 6,477 0.909 99.10 89.16 93.87 99.10 94.58 96.79
frog rule-based 6,533 0.917 97.83 88.86 93.13 97.83 94.26 96.02
spacy_lg UD-trained 7,199 1.010 94.82 95.91 95.36 94.63 97.96 96.27
stanza UD-trained 7,116 0.999 96.42 96.28 96.35 96.25 97.44 96.84

The raw ranking is almost entirely a guideline artefact. Remove the colon/semicolon boundaries and all four systems sit within 0.8 points (96.02–96.84); Alpino moves from last to a tie with Stanza, and has the highest boundary precision of any system either way — when Alpino posits a boundary, the reference agrees 99.1% of the time.

Tool to tool:

pair boundary F1 docs identical
alpino vs frog 97.60 82.69%

Two rule-based splitters that share neither code nor training data agree with each other far more than either agrees with the UD-trained pair. That is the strongest evidence here that the two camps are real and not an artefact of how the table is grouped.

Note also that the spread between tools (93–97.6) is far wider than for tokenisation (token F1 99.5–99.7). Sentence splitting, not tokenisation, is where these systems actually diverge.

Frog’s profile is high precision (97.83) and low recall (88.86) — it under-splits, producing 8% fewer sentences than the reference. Inspecting sentences_disagreements.csv, its misses are concentrated on:

  • Colons and semicolons. The reference splits after : and ;; neither rule-based system does. Of 624 boundaries the reference makes that neither Frog nor Alpino makes, 285 follow a colon and 85 a semicolon — 370, i.e. every colon/semicolon boundary in the reference is rejected by both. Against that, only 36 boundaries go the other way. This is one guideline decision, not a capability gap.
  • Closing quotes. Frog puts the boundary before a closing quote (Hoe ik het kon. || " Met dit... rather than kon." || Met dit...). This one is a genuine Frog error.
  • URLs. Frog splits inside them (...held-zonder-vaderland. || html).

Alignment bug found here

Document mode initially reported 341 unalignable tokens, all in one review containing a line consisting of a lone full stop. char_align had been searching unboundedly forward, so a single rewritten token matched a much later occurrence of itself and dragged the cursor past everything in between. The search is now bounded with a resync escape hatch; failures dropped from 341 to 1 in 115,142. Worth knowing because it would have scaled badly: ~0.15% of documents on a full-corpus run.

What to actually trust

sentences_independent_vs_reference.csv isolates the boundaries where both rule-based splitters agree with each other and disagree with the reference. Those decisions are made by machinery that owes the reference nothing, so they are the cases worth hand-checking first — and the natural seed for a gold segmentation set, which is the only thing that would turn any of this into accuracy.

6. Retraining spaCy’s lemmatizer on silver

nl_core_news_lg lemmatises with an EditTreeLemmatizer — a trained component, not a lookup table — so its OOV failures are a data problem and in principle fixable by retraining on in-domain silver. build_lemma_trainset.py builds the data (98,121 tokens train / 17,108 dev from Alpino silver, 99.7% of spaCy tokens aligned, split by review so no sentences leak between splits) and a config that retrains only the lemmatizer on top of frozen sourced components.

Training converged quickly and plateaued. The result, however, is a negative one worth stating plainly.

Read the baseline carefully

spacy train reports LEMMA_ACC = 73.92 at step 0. That is not stock spaCy. The config declares trainable_lemmatizer with factory, so a fresh component is initialised; at step 0 it is untrained and backoff = "orth" makes it return the surface form unchanged. 73.92% is the copy-the-form baseline. Stock nl_core_news_lg scores 93.17% against Alpino silver on held-out reviews.

Held-out vs training performance

Agreement with Alpino silver, punctuation excluded, dev = reviews never seen in training:

split bucket spacy_lg spacy_retrained
dev all 93.17% 93.97%
dev hapax 66.43% 65.04%
dev rare (2–5) 82.74% 84.10%
dev common (6+) 96.99% 97.91%
train all 93.53% 96.68%
train hapax 67.09% 77.48%
train rare (2–5) 81.85% 91.95%
train common (6+) 97.27% 98.93%

On reviews it trained on, the model gains 10.4 points on hapaxes. On reviews it did not, it loses 1.4. The overall held-out gain is +0.80 points. That gap is memorisation, not generalisation: 98k tokens teaches the model the specific rare words in the training reviews, and rare words by definition do not recur.

244 held-out tokens are fixed and 123 regressed. Inspecting them (retrained_lemma_fixed.csv, retrained_lemma_regressed.csv):

  • Most “fixes” are the model adopting Alpino’s convention rather than repairing an error — spelen → af_spelen, uitleggen → uit_leggen, verhaallijn → verhaal_lijn. Valuable if you want Alpino-style lemmas, but it is not error correction.
  • Genuine hallucination repairs do occur: dialoag → dialoog, Kelly's → Kelly.
  • The regressions are backoff = "orth" firing when no learned edit tree matches, so the model returns the surface form: gereden → gereden, schreven → schreven, staat → staat.

The flag-based view is circular here

Running compare_lemmas.py with the retrained model swapped in shows its unattested rate dropping 0.962% → 0.303%. That number should not be trusted: the flags judge a lemma partly by whether other tools propose it, and a model trained on Alpino silver agrees with Alpino by construction. The tell is that Frog’s unattested rate moved from 0.348% to 0.639% in the same run even though Frog did not change — the attestation universe shifted under it. Use the held-out table above instead; that is what eval_retrained_lemmatizer.py exists for.

How much silver would actually be needed

learning_curve.py trains the same configuration at five sizes and scores every model on the same held-out reviews, decomposing the result into coverage (test tokens whose form was seen in training) and generalisation (accuracy on forms never seen):

train tokens coverage acc_all acc_seen acc_unseen acc_hapax
4,300 70.8% 86.59 94.48 67.48 57.27
11,091 77.8% 90.38 95.98 70.79 59.44
25,485 83.0% 92.11 96.37 71.27 62.14
52,075 86.7% 93.34 96.69 71.44 64.93
97,819 89.3% 93.97 96.73 70.82 65.01

acc_unseen is flat. A 23× increase in training data moves it from 67.5 to 70.8 and the last step is negative. The entire headline gain comes from coverage rising 70.8% → 89.3%: the model is memorising this corpus’s vocabulary, not learning Dutch morphology it can apply to new words.

Vocabulary growth on this corpus fits Heaps’ law at V = 3.33 * N^0.743. That exponent is high — newspaper Dutch is nearer 0.6 — because reviews keep minting compounds, spelling variants, titles and names. Unseen-token mass therefore falls only as N^-0.257: a 100× larger silver corpus cuts it by about 3×. Projecting accuracy from the coverage model:

train tokens est. coverage est. accuracy
97,819 89.3% 93.97%
978,190 (10×) 94.1% 95.20%
9,781,900 (100×) 96.7% 95.88%

Conclusion: do not distil

Stock nl_core_news_lg already scores 93.17% against Alpino. Even 10M tokens of silver — 100× what was used here — reaches only ~95.9%, so the whole exercise is worth about 2.7 points of Alpino fidelity, and none of it on the rare vocabulary that motivated it.

The break-even does not exist, because the full Alpino run is affordable — though less comfortably than first estimated. The collection is 6,893,445 sentences over 670,748 reviews, ~106M tokens; at the measured 0.319 sentences/s per core it is 4.2 days on two 30-core VMs (§7), not the 1.5–3 days extrapolated from emulated timings. Reaching ~95.9% by distillation would still require Alpino-parsing 10M tokens — ~10% of the collection, around 10 hours of the same compute — to save four days while giving up four points of accuracy and carrying a second pipeline.

Parse everything with Alpino. Keep the distillation path only for a future case where new reviews must be annotated interactively or at high volume, where a ~96% model at 9,500 tokens/s is a reasonable fast pass. The scripts remain so the decision can be re-derived with different numbers:

make lemma-train SILVER=alpino && make lemma-eval
python src/parsers/learning_curve.py --silver alpino

7. Throughput

Measured on the production VMs

Two VMs, 30 cores and 375 GB each, native x86_64 in Docker, -veryfast, 200 sentences per shard across 30 shards per VM (57 shards completed):

pooled per-shard rate 0.319 sentences/s (11,400 sentences / 35,710 shard-seconds)
mean sentence length 15.4 tokens
sentences with skips > 0 3.6–3.7%
failed to parse 2 of 11,400
configuration rate full corpus (6.89M sentences)
1 VM, 30 shards 9.6 sentences/s 200 h (8.3 days)
2 VMs, 60 shards 19.2 sentences/s 100 h (4.2 days)

The full corpus is 6,893,445 sentences over 670,748 reviews, roughly 106M tokens at the measured mean length.

An earlier estimate in this report of 1–3 sentences/s per core, extrapolated from emulated timings on a Mac, was optimistic by 3–10×. Native throughput is ~0.32 sentences/s per core. Do not size a corpus run from emulated numbers.

The per-shard spread is small-sample noise

Shard times ranged 275–1397s for an identical 200 sentences each — a 5× spread that looks alarming and is not. Neither obvious cause holds: corr(tokens, seconds) = 0.37, and corr(skips, seconds) = 0.06 (the fastest shard had 6 timeouts, the slowest also 6).

Alpino’s per-sentence cost is heavy-tailed, and skips only counts sentences where it abandoned a spanning analysis; a sentence that burns 40s and parses successfully does not appear in that count. At 200 sentences per shard two or three such sentences dominate the total. At ~230k sentences per shard in a real run this averages out, which is why the projections above use the pooled rate rather than the slowest shard.

--user-max (default 60000 ms) is the lever if 4.2 days is too long: it bounds what any single sentence can cost, at the price of more partial parses.

Frog does not scale the same way everywhere

Frog’s per-process rate is ~275–300 sentences/s, but how it scales across concurrent processes turned out to be machine-dependent, and badly so on one of the VMs. Measured with scaling_frog.sh, 150–200 sentences per shard:

shards Mac aggregate (sent/s) Mac efficiency VM3 aggregate VM3 efficiency
1 300 100% 275 100%
2 600 100% — —
4 1,091 90.9% 167 15.2%
6 1,543 85.7% — —
8 1,882 78.4% — —
22 — — 36 0.6%

On the Mac, throughput scales nearly linearly with a gentle tail-off. On VM3, aggregate throughput falls as shards are added – one process does more total work than four, which do more than twenty-two. That is not oversubscription, which flattens a curve rather than inverting it.

The shape fits cache and memory-bandwidth contention: Frog’s tagger and MBMA are TiMBL memory-based classifiers doing pointer-chasing nearest-neighbour lookups over large in-memory models. One process keeps its working set largely resident; several evict each other and every lookup falls through to RAM. The degradation is uniform across shards, which is what a shared bottleneck looks like and what initially made it easy to mistake for a slow installation.

The practical lesson generalises beyond Frog: measure the scaling curve before choosing a shard count, and pick the count with the highest aggregate rather than the highest core count. make scaling-frog does this in a few minutes.

At 8 shards on the Mac – 1,882 sentences/s aggregate – the full 6.89M-sentence corpus takes about 1 hour. Against Alpino’s 4.2 days on two 30-core VMs that is a factor of ~100, and it is what makes the hybrid in §8 a clear choice rather than a compromise.

Operational notes

Four things cost real time before any of the above was measurable, all of them worth knowing before a long run:

  • Run queue, not memory, is the thing to watch. A collapsed rate with zero swap and zero iowait but r several times nproc means oversubscription. This happened when killed runs left orphaned shards behind: vmstat showed r = 155 on 30 cores and the rate fell to 0.106 sentences/s, a third of the clean figure.
  • Killing a driver orphans its shards. The shards are backgrounded and each spawns Alpino; killing an ancestor leaves the children running and holding memory. The drivers now trap INT/TERM and signal their process group, and for the Docker path the container is named and explicitly killed — terminating the docker run client alone does not stop the container. make kill-runs recovers from a kill -9.
  • Loading the input must stream. Materialising all rows before sharding costs 2.2 GB per process at corpus scale — 66 GB across 30 shards, before Alpino. Streaming and discarding non-shard rows brings that to ~195 MB.
  • Docker Desktop’s memory ceiling binds on a Mac but not on Linux. At the 3.8 GB default, 8 Alpino containers OOM-killed each other and throughput fell to ~0.05 sentences/s; raising it to ~31 GB gave an ~8× speedup.

Alpino makes no use of the A10 GPUs — it is CPU-only. Those are only useful for Stanza, spaCy-trf, and lemmatizer retraining.

8. Recommendation

For a corpus of Dutch book reviews where POS, lemma and morphology all matter:

  1. Alpino where syntax is actually needed, sharded one process per core. At 4.2 days for the full corpus on two VMs it is affordable but not free, so if dependency structure is only needed for part of the analysis, parse that part. It has the most central tokenisation, the second-lowest unattested rate, the richest morphology, and it supplies compound decomposition in the lemma. The cost is ~3 orders of magnitude less throughput than spaCy, which is affordable for a one-off corpus build and not for anything interactive.
  2. Frog over everything. At ~4,400 tokens/s per core, and ~1,880 sentences/s aggregate on 8 cores, it covers the whole 106M-token corpus in about an hour — roughly 100× faster than Alpino — and supplies POS, lemma and full morpheme decomposition. With a fixed sentence split the case for preferring Alpino rests almost entirely on the dependency parse; on lemmas the two agree 94.2% exactly and 96.9% modulo convention, with no evidence either is better. Frog is also the only system here independent of the other three, making it the most useful second opinion.
  3. Stanza or spaCy on GPU over the same corpus for a UD view and a cheap disagreement signal; sentences where the fast and slow pipelines disagree are the ones worth looking at.
  4. Do not retrain spaCy’s lemmatizer on Alpino silver for this corpus (§6). The learning curve shows the gain is coverage-driven and caps out ~2.7 points above stock spaCy, while the full Alpino run is affordable outright.
  5. DeepFrog was considered and rejected. It is a separate project from Frog (Rust, cargo install, needs libtorch), its GitHub repository was archived in September 2026 and marked suspended, and it ships POS and NER models only — no lemmatiser, no morphological analysis, no parser. Its POS is ~97.4 F1 (BERTje) against Frog’s ~96–98 on the same CGN tagset, so adopting it would trade the lemmas and MBMA morphology that Frog is here for against roughly a point of tagging accuracy. The models are on HuggingFace (proycon/bert-pos-cased-deepfrog-nld) and usable from transformers directly, should a POS-only need ever arise.
  6. Build a gold dev set. Everything here is agreement. A few hundred hand-corrected sentences would convert all of it into accuracy. lemma_disagreements.csv and lemma_unattested_examples.csv are sorted rarest-first and make a reasonable sampling frame.
